In-House vs. Outsourced Medical Billing:
The Real Cost Comparison

Most practices do this math wrong. They compare a biller's salary to a billing service's percentage and stop there. But in-house billing carries costs that never show up in that calculation — and in a year of Medicare cuts, the gap matters more than ever. Here's the full picture.

The Question Every Practice Eventually Asks

At some point, almost every practice manager or physician owner sits down and asks: would we save money doing billing ourselves, or should we pay someone else to do it? It seems like it should be a simple math problem. It is not.

The salary comparison — a biller's annual wages versus a service's percentage of collections — is where most practices start and stop. That math sometimes favors in-house billing on paper. But it leaves out the costs that don't have a line item in your QuickBooks: lost revenue from denied claims that never get appealed, the cost of training a replacement when your biller leaves, the billing software license, the clearinghouse fees, and the revenue leakage from codes that were never optimized.

Add those in, and the picture changes — especially for practices with under $2 million in annual collections, which is most independent practices in the country.

That range — $73,000 to $107,000 per year — is what in-house billing actually costs before you factor in what the billing operation is not collecting. And that second number is where things get interesting.

The Hidden Cost: Revenue Leakage

A solo biller managing a full-volume practice faces a fundamental capacity problem. Claims need to go out, eligibility needs to be verified, and denials need to be followed up — all at the same time. When something has to give, it's usually denial management.

Industry data consistently shows that in-house billing at small practices leaves 3–8% of collectible revenue on the table annually through a combination of:

  • Denied claims that are never appealed (payers count on this)
  • Undercoded E/M visits due to time pressure or uncertainty
  • Missed modifiers that reduce legitimate payment
  • Credentialing gaps that create non-covered claim periods
  • Patient balance write-offs that could have been collected with proper follow-up
What 5% leakage actually costs

On a practice with $800,000 in annual Medicare and insurance collections, a 5% revenue leakage rate is $40,000 per year — silently walking out the door. That's real money that never showed up as a line item expense, so it rarely gets noticed until a billing audit.

The breakeven point

For most small practices under $1.5M in annual collections, outsourcing at a competitive rate is cheaper than in-house billing even on paper — and that's before accounting for revenue leakage. Above $2M, the math becomes closer, but the operational advantages of outsourcing (no HR, no coverage gaps, specialist depth) often still win.

When In-House Billing Makes Sense

In-house billing genuinely works well in a few specific situations. It is not the right answer for most independent practices, but it is the right answer for some.

  • Very high volume, single-specialty practices — a large cardiology or orthopedic group with $3M+ in collections and a dedicated billing manager and team can often run lean in-house. The fixed cost of staff is absorbed across a large volume base.
  • Hospital systems and large group practices — with an internal billing department, compliance officer, and dedicated denial management team, in-house billing can be highly efficient. This isn't the same as one biller at a solo practice.
  • Practices with highly specialized billing needs — certain niche specialties with unusual coding requirements sometimes benefit from someone physically present who understands the specific clinical workflows.

Signs Your Current Billing Setup Is Costing You

Your denial rate is above 5%

Industry benchmark for a well-run billing operation is under 5%. If yours is higher, you're funding payers' cash flow instead of your own.

AR days are over 45

Average days in accounts receivable above 45 means cash is sitting in limbo. Healthy practices run 30–40 AR days on clean specialties.

Biller turnover in the last 2 years

Every time a biller leaves, there's a gap period where follow-up stalls and denials age out. The revenue loss is often invisible but real.

You can't tell your collection rate by payer

If your billing operation can't pull a clean report of collections by payer and by procedure code, you're flying blind on your biggest revenue levers.

Your biller handles front desk too

Combining billing with front-desk duties is one of the most common and expensive billing mistakes. Neither function gets done well when attention is split.

Credentialing is months behind

Providers seeing patients before credentialing is complete are generating $0 claims. This is one of the most expensive billing errors a practice can make.

What to Ask a Billing Service Before You Sign

Not all billing services are equal. The percentage rate is not the most important variable. Here are the questions that actually reveal whether a service will perform:

  • What is your average clean claim rate? (Xpert: 98%)
  • What is your denial follow-up policy and turnaround time?
  • Do I get a dedicated biller or is my account pooled? (Xpert: dedicated biller per account)
  • Who handles credentialing and payer enrollment?
  • Can I see sample reports — AR by payer, collection rate by CPT code?
  • Are your billers US-based? (Xpert: 100% US-based team)
  • What is your contract term and what does it take to leave?

A billing service that can't answer these questions clearly is not a billing service you want running your revenue cycle.
