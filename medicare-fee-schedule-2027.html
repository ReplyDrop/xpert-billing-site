Here's exactly what's changing — and the billing steps that protect your income.

The 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule takes effect January 1. The 2.5% Congressional bonus that protected physician pay in 2026 has expired, and no last-minute legislation has passed. Practices that don't adjust their billing workflows before the new year will feel this immediately in their January remittances.

What's Changing on January 1

Every year, CMS resets what it pays for every Medicare-covered service. The 2027 rule is a particularly difficult one — a compounding set of changes that hits procedure-heavy specialties hardest while making little adjustment for the actual cost of running a practice.

The Conversion Factor Cut

The Medicare conversion factor — the dollar multiplier applied to every relative value unit (RVU) — drops to approximately $32.59 for non-APM physicians in 2027, down from $33.40 in 2026. That is a 2.31% cut on every Medicare service you bill, before specialty-specific adjustments are layered on top.

The cut comes primarily from the expiration of a one-year 2.5% increase that Congress passed for 2026. Without a new act of Congress before January 1, those dollars disappear. Physician groups have been lobbying hard — but as of now, no fix has been enacted.

The New Same-Day Billing Rule

This is the change most practices are underprepared for. Under the 2027 rule, when an E/M (office visit) is billed on the same day as a procedure with a 0-, 10-, or 90-day global period, the lower-value service is reimbursed at 50% — not 100%.

In plain terms: if a surgeon sees a patient, decides to perform a procedure, and bills both the visit and the procedure on the same date of service, one of those two claims will be paid at half rate. For high-volume procedure specialties like dermatology, orthopedics, and ENT, this single rule change accounts for a significant portion of their projected revenue decline.

What this means in practice

A dermatologist who sees and biopsies a patient in the same visit is now only collecting 50% on either the E/M or the biopsy, depending on which carries lower value. Multiply that across dozens of patients per week, and the revenue impact is substantial — even before the conversion factor cut.

End of Traditional MIPS

The 2027 rule begins winding down traditional MIPS (Merit-based Incentive Payment System), with a full sunset expected by 2031. Practices still optimizing for MIPS performance should begin understanding the successor programs now, as quality reporting requirements and bonus structures will shift materially over the next few years.

G2211 Becomes a Modifier

The complex/chronic care add-on code G2211 transitions to a percentage-based modifier in 2027. For primary care and internal medicine, this can represent a 16% increase on associated E/M payments — a rare piece of good news in an otherwise difficult rule. Practices that haven't been using G2211 should evaluate whether they qualify.

What This Means for Your Billing

Revenue cuts from CMS don't have to be absorbed dollar-for-dollar. The practices that fare best in tight-reimbursement years are the ones with the cleanest billing operations — because every dollar CMS is willing to pay, they actually collect.

Here's where the gaps show up:

  • Same-day claim errors: The new E/M + procedure rule is already generating claim rejections at clearinghouses that haven't updated their edits. A biller who doesn't know the rule will keep submitting claims the old way and wonder why they're being denied.
  • Under-coded E/M visits: With the conversion factor down, the difference between a 99213 and a 99214 is more meaningful than ever. Accurate E/M coding based on documented complexity is now a revenue protection strategy, not just a compliance exercise.
  • G2211 left on the table: Primary care practices that qualify for G2211 and aren't using it are leaving the one available offset on the table. This requires both documentation and a biller who knows when to append it.
  • Slow denial follow-up: Medicare Advantage plans are using the fee schedule changes as cover to introduce new denial logic. A claim that passed last year may now require different supporting documentation.
  • Credentialing gaps: Providers not properly enrolled with CMS are receiving $0 for Medicare patients, regardless of the fee schedule.

6 Billing Steps to Take Before January 1

1

Audit your same-day billing patterns now

Pull a 90-day report of all claims where an E/M was billed alongside a procedure on the same DOS. Identify which of those will be affected by the 50% rule and calculate the projected revenue impact. This tells you the size of the hole before January 1.

2

Update your charge capture workflow

Your billing team needs clear, written guidance on how to handle same-day E/M + procedure encounters in 2027. Which service gets billed at full, which gets the modifier, and when does documentation support billing both? Don't wait for the first denial to work this out.

3

Review E/M coding against documentation

With a lower conversion factor, accurate E/M level selection directly protects revenue. Have your billing team run a sample audit of your most common visit types — are the codes matching the documented medical decision-making? Downcoding is a common, invisible revenue leak.

4

Evaluate G2211 eligibility

If you're in primary care, internal medicine, or another longitudinal care specialty, work with your biller to determine whether G2211 applies to your patient panel. When properly documented and appended, it increases the associated E/M payment by 16% — a meaningful offset in a cut year.

5

Confirm all provider enrollments are current

Any Medicare provider enrollment that lapsed, changed addresses, or added a new location needs to be verified before January 1. CMS does not backpay for dates of service prior to an effective enrollment date, no matter how clean the claim is.

6

Project your 2027 Medicare revenue by procedure

Use CMS's published conversion factor and RVU values to model what your top 20 procedure codes will pay in 2027 versus 2026. The number will be uncomfortable, but knowing it in November gives you time to adjust — increasing Medicare Advantage contracting, reviewing fee schedules with commercial payers, or adjusting your financial planning before the year starts.

The Bigger Picture: Reimbursement Compression Is Not Going Away

The 2027 cuts follow a pattern. Medicare's conversion factor has declined in real terms in most years since 2001. Inflation in the cost of delivering care — staff wages, malpractice, supplies, rent — has consistently outpaced what CMS pays. MGMA and physician groups continue to call it unsustainable, but Congress has shown little appetite for a permanent fix.

What this means operationally: practices that are still running billing in-house with 2015-era workflows are paying a hidden tax. Every missed modifier, every delayed appeal, every under-coded visit is money that the fee schedule reduction didn't take — but your billing operation did. The practices that survive and grow in this environment are the ones running billing as a precision operation, not an administrative afterthought.

The Bottom Line

The 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule is not good news for most practices. The conversion factor cut is real, the same-day billing rule will catch unprepared billers off guard, and certain specialties are looking at the worst reimbursement environment in years for their core procedures.

But the practices that go into 2027 with clean billing operations, accurate coding, and fast denial resolution will out-collect their peers — because the fee schedule sets a ceiling, not a floor. How much of that ceiling you actually reach depends entirely on how well your revenue cycle runs.
